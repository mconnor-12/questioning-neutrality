What kind of historical learning program does the Regents in U.S. and Global History actually deliver to New York State students, and does it live up to its promise of coherence and consistency?

Author Marie Connor
Institution Teachers College, Columbia
Site Dream Charter High School, Bronx
Corpus 17 sittings, 49,345 student-by-item responses
The argument, in opening

The Regents is a map the state issues of its own historical territory.

Borges wrote of an empire whose cartographers drew a map at the scale of the empire itself, until the map became the territory. The New York State Regents in U.S. and Global History is that kind of map. Students read it. They are tested on it. The map says it is the territory.

This project began in a Bronx classroom where, sitting at students' shoulders for years, the seam between what the exam claimed to measure and what my students actually knew began to pull. What the Regents calls historical knowledge is in part a measurement of fluency with one particular map, drawn at one particular scale, by one particular set of cartographers. The library teachers build alongside it is the territory the map cannot show.

Comparability is asserted, not delivered. Consistency is claimed at the institutional level and contradicted at the structural level. Inside that bounded reality, teachers have specific, durable space to draw the territory the map cannot show.

This site presents the four sub-questions that ground the argument, the empirical findings each one surfaces, and the implications for both research and teaching practice. The deeper analysis behind each finding is one click away.

The argument, in a sentence

The Regents asserts a coherent program experienced consistently across New York State, and the structural evidence shows that promise is partial at best. Inside that bounded reality, teachers have specific, durable space to draw the territory the map cannot show.
